# Starting out - the part no calculator tells you

This guide assumes no country. Wherever you live, the sequence below
is roughly the same, and getting it in the right order saves months.

The simulator in this portal answers *what your money could become*.
This page answers *what you actually have to do first*.

---

## Before you invest anything

**1. Clear expensive debt first.**
A credit card at 30% is a guaranteed 30% loss. No fund reliably
beats that. Paying it off is the highest-certainty return available
to you, and it is available today.

**2. Hold cash you can reach.**
Three to six months of essential spending, somewhere boring and
instant. This is not an investment and should not be measured like
one. Its whole job is to stop you selling investments at the worst
possible moment - which is exactly when emergencies happen.

**3. Know what the money is for.**
"Retirement in thirty years" and "a house deposit in four" are
different problems with different answers. Money you need in under
five years generally should not be in equities at all, because the
one thing markets reliably do over short periods is move against
you when you need them not to.

---

## Opening an account

The mechanics differ by country; the questions do not.

- **Identity verification.** Every jurisdiction requires it. Expect
  to prove who you are, where you live, and often where you pay
  tax. Have documents ready before you start, not halfway through.
- **A tax identity number.** Whatever your country calls it.
- **A bank account that can send and receive.** Usually one in the
  same country as the investment account.
- **Tax residency declarations.** Most countries now exchange
  financial account information automatically. Declaring your tax
  residency truthfully at the outset is far easier than correcting
  it later.

## Choosing what to buy

This portal deliberately takes no view on specific funds. Three
things it *can* say, because they are arithmetic rather than
opinion:

**Costs compound too.** An expense ratio is charged on the whole
balance every year. Over thirty years the difference between 0.2%
and 1.5% is not 1.3% - it is a large fraction of everything you
end up with.
Put both into the Advanced Simulator and look.

**Diversification is free.** Spreading across many holdings reduces
the risk of any one failing without reducing expected return. It is
the only thing in investing that is genuinely free.

**Time in the market beats timing it.** Not a slogan - a measurable
effect. The Historical & Risk Lab replays the same plan from every
start month of real index history, and the spread between the best
and worst is smaller than the cost of sitting out waiting for a
good one.

## Setting it up so you don't have to think

- **Automate the contribution.** A standing instruction that runs
  on payday removes the monthly decision, and the monthly decision
  is where plans die.
- **Increase it when your income does.** This is the single most
  effective habit available to you, and the Compare Journeys screen
  will show you why: a step-up that looks trivial in year one
  compounds for every year after it.
- **Then leave it alone.** Checking a thirty-year plan weekly tells
  you nothing about thirty years and a great deal about the last
  seven days.

---

## What to do when markets fall

Decide now, in writing, what you will do - because deciding during
a fall is how people sell at the bottom.

For most people with a long horizon the answer is *keep buying*.
A falling market means your automatic contribution buys more units
for the same money. That is not a consolation; it is the mechanism
by which long-horizon investors do well.

Use the Historical & Risk Lab before you need it. Seeing what your
plan looks like in a bad decade, while nothing is actually falling,
is worth a great deal when one is.
